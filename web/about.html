Who we are

submarinedocent.org is built and maintained by two volunteer docents who give tours aboard a World War II submarine: Irving Greisman, who qualified in submarines aboard USS Tusk in 1969, and Dwight Naset, who wrote the glossary. Visitors ask us the same questions every day. This site is where we answer them.

Hear the welcome (1 minute 15 seconds)

Hi, I’m Irving Greisman. Welcome aboard.

In 1969 I earned my dolphins, the badge of a qualified submariner, aboard USS Tusk, a diesel boat and close cousin of the World War II submarines on this site.

Today I give tours aboard a World War II submarine, and visitors ask the same questions every day. How did they sleep? How was the food? This site is where fellow docent Dwight Naset and I answer them. Dwight also wrote the glossary, so when a term like stern plane stops you cold, he’s got you covered.

Start with a few short answers, or go deep, from batteries and torpedoes to what the cooks served at midnight. Eternal Patrol honors the boats and crews that never came home.

And when you’re ready for the real thing, our museum guide will point you to the nearest boat. Nothing beats squeezing through a hatch yourself.

Didn’t find your question? Send it to us.

Thanks for coming aboard. Let’s go below.

What you will find here

  • Straightforward answers to common questions, from storage and machinery to fear, food, and daily routine. Browse the FAQs.
  • Detailed technical explanations covering diesel-electric systems, batteries, sonar, periscopes, torpedoes, and more.
  • Stories about the people, including officers, enlisted men, cooks, radiomen, torpedomen, and everyone in between.
  • Eternal Patrol, honoring the submarines and sailors lost in service, with an account of each boat's final mission.
  • A glossary, so terms like trim pump, conning tower, and battle surface are one tap away.
  • Submarine museums open to the public, so you can go aboard one yourself.

Have a question we have not answered? Ask the Docent, out loud or in writing, or send us a question or suggestion. Your questions help us decide what to cover next, both here and in future videos.

Why are women not mentioned in the FAQs?

We have been asked this, and it is a fair question.

Women did not serve on United States submarines until 2011. Other navies integrated women earlier, including Norway in the mid-1980s, followed by Australia, Canada, Spain, and others in the 1990s and 2000s. The United States was slower to do so primarily because of berthing and privacy constraints on older submarine designs.

The museum boats and their stewards

Fourteen WWII fleet submarines are open to the public as museum ships across the United States, from Pampanito in San Francisco to Cod in Cleveland, Bowfin in Pearl Harbor, and Silversides in Muskegon. Each is preserved and restored by a museum or nonprofit that raises the money and does the work. This site draws on all of them as subject matter. See the full list on our Museums page.
